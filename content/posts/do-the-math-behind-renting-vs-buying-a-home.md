A house is the biggest purchase most people ever make, and most people decide to buy one for reasons that have nothing to do with the numbers. A friend just bought. Rent went up again. It feels like the responsible, grown-up thing to do. None of that is wrong, exactly, but none of it tells you whether buying actually makes financial sense for your situation this year. The buy-vs-rent decision is a math problem before it's a lifestyle decision, and treating it that way is how you avoid a five-figure mistake.

Accord Advisors works with individuals and business owners across Bloomington and Indianapolis on exactly this kind of decision, alongside tax planning and outsourced accounting for medical, dental, and optometry practices. The framework is the same whether someone is buying a $1.2 million practice building or a $260,000 starter home: put both scenarios on paper, compare real cash flow, and let the numbers argue with your gut.

## Why this is a balance sheet decision, not a gut decision

Renting and buying are both financial products with a monthly payment attached. One builds equity slowly and comes with maintenance, property tax, and insurance obligations you can't opt out of. The other has a payment that can rise every year but comes with no repair bills, no property tax, and no capital tied up in a down payment. Comparing them by "gut feel" ignores the fact that money put into a down payment stops being available for anything else, including a retirement account or a taxable brokerage account that could be compounding for a decade.

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances shows homeowners carry substantially higher median net worth than renters, but that gap reflects decades of forced savings through mortgage paydown and price appreciation, not proof that buying is always the smarter move in year one or year five ([Federal Reserve SCF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf)). Net worth statistics describe outcomes after 15 or 20 years. They don't tell you what happens if you sell in year three.

## Run the numbers side by side

Here's a simple comparison for a $280,000 home in the Bloomington area, financed with 10% down at a 6.75% rate on a 30-year fixed mortgage, against a comparable rental at $1,500 a month.

| Cost category | Buying | Renting |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly payment (principal + interest) | ~$1,630 | $1,500 |
| Property tax (est. 1% annually) | ~$233/mo | $0 |
| Homeowners insurance | ~$100/mo | $0 (renters insurance ~$15/mo) |
| Maintenance (1-2% of value annually) | ~$280/mo | $0 |
| Down payment + closing costs (upfront) | ~$36,400 | Security deposit only |
| Total monthly cash outflow | ~$2,243 | ~$1,515 |

That's roughly a $728 monthly gap in year one, before any tax benefit and before home value appreciation. Multiply that gap by 12 and you get $8,736 a year that could instead go into an IRA, a 529 plan, or a taxable investment account if you rent and invest the difference. Whether buying still wins depends on how much the home appreciates, how long you stay, and what that invested difference would have earned instead.

## The costs both sides tend to underestimate

Renters underestimate how much control they're giving up. A landlord can decline to renew a lease, sell the property, or raise rent 8% in a single year with 30 or 60 days' notice depending on the lease terms. That instability has a cost even if it never shows up on a spreadsheet.

Buyers underestimate two things almost every time: transaction costs and maintenance. Selling a home typically costs 6-8% of the sale price in agent commissions and closing costs, which means a homeowner who sells within two or three years often loses money even if the home appreciated in value. Maintenance is the other blind spot. A furnace replacement runs $4,000 to $7,000. A roof runs $8,000 to $15,000. Budgeting 1-2% of home value per year for upkeep is a reasonable rule of thumb, and most first-time buyers budget nothing at all.

The mortgage interest deduction gets brought up in almost every buy-vs-rent conversation, and it matters less than people think. You only benefit from it if your itemized deductions, mortgage interest included, exceed the standard deduction, which is $29,200 for married couples filing jointly in 2024. For a lot of buyers with a modest mortgage balance, the standard deduction already wins, meaning the "tax break" on the mortgage doesn't change their tax bill at all ([IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505)).

## When the math tends to favor buying

Buying starts to win financially when a few conditions line up together: you plan to stay in the home at least five years, your monthly buying cost isn't dramatically higher than renting a comparable place, and you have enough saved to cover the down payment without draining your emergency fund. The five-year mark matters because it takes that long, in most markets, for appreciation and equity paydown to outweigh the 6-8% you'll give up in selling costs. Physicians, optometrists, and dentists relocating for a new practice often buy too soon, before they know whether the position will stick, and that's usually where the math breaks down.

## When the math tends to favor renting

Renting wins when your time horizon is uncertain, when the local price-to-rent ratio is high (meaning homes cost a lot relative to what they'd rent for), or when buying would require draining savings you'd rather keep liquid for a business investment, tuition, or retirement contributions. It also wins for people early in a career who expect to relocate for a job. A service business owner scaling from a home office to a leased space in Bloomington may be better off renting a house for another two years and putting that capital into the business instead.

## Common Questions

**Is renting actually better than buying financially?**
It depends on your time horizon and local price-to-rent ratio, not on a universal rule. Renting tends to come out ahead financially if you'll stay less than five years, if buying costs meaningfully more per month than renting, or if you'd otherwise invest the down payment somewhere with a strong expected return.

**What's a reasonable rent vs buy calculator formula to use?**
Compare total monthly cash outflow (mortgage principal, interest, property tax, insurance, and 1-2% of home value annually for maintenance) against rent plus what the down payment and monthly savings difference would earn if invested instead. Then factor in 6-8% selling costs if you expect to move within five years.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying worth it?**
Only if your total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, which is $29,200 for married couples filing jointly in 2024. Many buyers with moderate mortgage balances don't clear that threshold, so the deduction doesn't lower their tax bill at all ([IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505)).

**How long should I plan to stay in a home before buying makes sense?**
Five years is a reasonable minimum in most markets. That's roughly how long it takes for equity paydown and appreciation to offset the 6-8% you'll typically pay in selling costs.
