Most insurance content reads like a shopping list: here's term life, here's disability, here's an umbrella policy, buy all three and sleep well. That advice ignores the only question that matters. How much of your net worth, your income, or your practice's revenue is actually exposed if something goes wrong? Insurance isn't a virtue you accumulate. It's a hedge against a specific, calculable loss, and once you size that loss correctly, the right coverage amount usually becomes obvious.

We see this misstep constantly with optometry and dental practice owners in Bloomington and Indianapolis: a $400,000 umbrella policy sitting next to a malpractice tail gap that could actually bankrupt the practice, or a life insurance policy sized for someone's income in 2015 that never got updated after two kids and a mortgage refinance. The fix isn't more insurance. It's insurance matched to the number that's actually at risk.

## Insurance is a math problem, not a checklist

Every insurance decision starts with the same three inputs: what you'd lose, how likely it is, and what you can afford to absorb yourself. If your household net worth is $150,000, a $2 million umbrella policy is probably overkill relative to your actual liability exposure. If it's $2.5 million and you own rental property or serve on a nonprofit board, that same policy might be underpriced protection for a genuine risk.

The same logic applies to a practice. A solo optometrist with $180,000 in annual revenue and no employees carries a different liability profile than a four-doctor group with $3 million in revenue, a lease, and a staff of twelve. Coverage decisions should track those numbers, not a generic list of "things a business should carry."

## What personal risk really costs on paper

Start by putting real numbers next to each major personal risk category. This is the exercise we walk through with clients before recommending any change to their coverage:

| Risk | What's exposed | Rough sizing approach |
|---|---|---|
| Premature death | Income replacement for dependents, mortgage payoff, education costs | 10 to 15 times annual income, adjusted for existing savings and debt |
| Disability | Ongoing income if you can't work | 60 to 70% of gross income replacement through age 65 |
| Liability lawsuits | Assets beyond what auto/home policies cover | Umbrella policy sized to match net worth, typically $1M to $5M |
| Long-term care | Retirement savings depletion in later years | Varies widely; often addressed at ages 55 to 65, not sooner |

A 42-year-old dentist in Avon with a $340,000 mortgage, two kids headed to college, and $900,000 in retirement and investment accounts has a very different life insurance need than a 61-year-old with the mortgage paid off and kids through school. Yet both might be carrying the same policy they bought at 35 because nobody revisited it. Net worth and dependents change. Coverage should change with them.

## Where practice owners get coverage backwards

Business insurance is where we see the most money wasted and the most real exposure ignored, often in the same practice. A few patterns show up again and again across the optometry, dental, and medical clients we work with:

**Malpractice tail coverage gets underfunded.** When a provider leaves a practice or retires, tail coverage to protect against claims filed after the fact can run $10,000 to $30,000 or more depending on specialty and years in practice. Practices that don't plan for this cost get blindsided at exactly the wrong moment, usually during a partner buyout or sale.

**Business overhead expense insurance gets skipped.** This one pays your practice's fixed costs, rent, staff payroll, loan payments, if you're disabled and can't see patients. A solo optometrist without it is one bad diagnosis away from a practice that can't cover its own overhead for six months.

**Key person and buy-sell funding get treated as optional.** If your practice has two or three owners and no funded buy-sell agreement, a death or disability among partners becomes a cash-flow crisis, not just a legal one. Life insurance can fund the buyout so the surviving owner isn't scrambling to borrow against the practice to pay out an estate. We've written more on structuring buyouts properly in our piece on [business valuation triggers](/resources/when-and-why-every-business-owner-needs-a-business-valuation), which pairs directly with this kind of planning.

Churches face a parallel version of this problem. Directors and officers coverage, property coverage on aging buildings, and workers' compensation for part-time and volunteer staff all need to be sized against the church's actual budget and risk profile, not a boilerplate denomination template. Our [staffing and compliance guidance for churches](/industries/churches/staffing-and-compliance) covers the payroll side of that exposure in more detail.

## Sizing coverage without guessing

A disciplined process looks like this:

1. **Calculate your net worth exposure.** List assets that a lawsuit or judgment could reach. This is your floor for umbrella coverage.
2. **Calculate income replacement need.** Multiply take-home pay by years of dependency (until kids are independent or a spouse reaches retirement age) and subtract existing savings.
3. **Separate personal risk from business risk.** A practice's malpractice and overhead exposure should be sized off practice revenue and fixed costs, not the owner's personal balance sheet.
4. **Revisit every 24 months, or at a trigger event.** Selling a rental property, buying into a practice, or adding a partner all change the math. If you're planning a sale of a business, rental, or large asset this year, coverage amounts, and the timing of any policy changes, should be part of that conversation. We cover the broader planning checklist in [our post on selling a business or large asset](/resources/selling-a-business-rental-or-large-asset-this-year-call-your-cpa-before-you-sign).

This is the same discipline we bring to a client's monthly financials: numbers first, decisions second. An insurance needs analysis isn't a form you fill out once. It's a recalculation you run whenever the underlying risk changes.

## Business insurance and tax deductibility

Most ordinary and necessary business insurance premiums, including malpractice, property, and business overhead expense coverage, are deductible business expenses. Life insurance where the business is a direct or indirect beneficiary generally is not. The rules get specific fast, and [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) lays out exactly which premiums qualify and which don't. Getting this wrong on a return is an easy way to trigger an adjustment down the road, which is one more reason coverage decisions and tax planning shouldn't happen in separate conversations.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do you actually need?**
Enough to cover the specific dollar amount you'd lose in the worst realistic scenario, not a round number pulled from a generic checklist. That means calculating income replacement needs against dependents and debt, liability exposure against net worth, and business interruption costs against fixed overhead, then buying coverage sized to those numbers.

**What types of insurance actually protect financial stability, versus which ones are optional?**
Life insurance, disability insurance, and liability coverage (auto, home, umbrella) protect core financial stability for most households. Business owners add malpractice or professional liability, business overhead expense, and key person or buy-sell funding. Coverage like extended warranties or low-deductible policies on small losses you could absorb yourself usually isn't worth the premium.

**Is there a business insurance coverage checklist that applies to every optometry or dental practice?**
No single checklist fits every practice, because the right coverage depends on revenue, staff count, ownership structure, and whether a buy-sell agreement exists. A four-doctor group with a shared lease has different exposure than a solo practitioner working out of a rented suite, even in the same specialty.

**How often should a personal risk management plan be reviewed?**
Every 24 months at minimum, and immediately after a major life or business event: a new mortgage, a new partner, the sale of a property or practice, or a significant jump in net worth. Coverage that made sense five years ago rarely still fits today's numbers.

If your coverage hasn't been recalculated since you bought it, it's probably wrong in one direction or the other.
