## Why related entity transactions attract IRS scrutiny

Many Bloomington and Indianapolis business owners run more than one legal entity. An optometry practice paired with a separate real estate LLC that owns the building. A dental practice with its own management company. A service business paying a related staffing entity for labor. Whenever money moves between entities you control, the IRS wants confirmation that the price was fair.

Transfer pricing is simply the term for how much one related entity charges another for rent, management services, equipment use, or shared staff. When a practice pays below-market rent to an LLC you also own, or a holding company charges an inflated management fee, the IRS sees a chance for income and deductions to shift between entities in ways that reduce overall tax. That's why these transactions get flagged: they weren't negotiated the way a deal with a stranger would be.

## Common situations where transfer pricing rules apply

Related entity transactions show up in ordinary operating decisions, not just complicated corporate structures. If any of these sound familiar, transfer pricing rules likely already apply to your business:

- Management fees paid from an operating practice to a holding company for administrative oversight
- Rent paid by an optometry, dental, or medical practice to a real estate LLC held by the same owner
- A bookkeeper or office manager splitting time across two related businesses without a documented allocation
- Equipment or vehicles shared between a service business and an affiliated entity
- Loans made between commonly owned entities without a market interest rate attached
- A church-affiliated school or daycare sharing staff or facilities with the congregation

None of these arrangements are wrong on their own. The problem starts when the amounts charged don't reflect what an unrelated party would reasonably pay, and there's no record showing how the number was set.

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-right | image: arms-length-rent-example.jpg | alt: "Accountant explaining fair market rent calculation to a client" | query: "accountant explaining calculation to client" -->
## The arm's length standard explained simply

The standard the IRS applies to related entity pricing has a formal name, but the idea is straightforward: charge what an unrelated party would charge in the same deal.

Say your optometry practice rents its space from an LLC you also own. If a stranger leasing comparable space in Bloomington would pay $28 per square foot, your practice should pay something close to that, not $12 and not $45. The same logic applies to management fees, equipment rental, or staff-sharing arrangements between your entities. The question is never whether related parties can transact with each other. They can, and often should. The question is whether the price would survive a side-by-side comparison to a deal between two unrelated business owners.

## Risks of getting intercompany pricing wrong

Undocumented or unreasonable pricing between related entities carries real consequences, but they're manageable ones when you know what to watch for:

- The IRS can reclassify income or expenses between entities, changing which one owes tax and how much
- Deductions tied to inflated management fees or rent can be disallowed entirely
- Penalties and interest accrue on any underpayment traced back to the reallocation
- Weak documentation makes an otherwise defensible arrangement look like an audit target

None of this requires a large legal team to fix. It requires consistent documentation and pricing that reflects market terms, set up before a return is filed rather than reconstructed after a notice arrives.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Transfer pricing and related entities what business owners need to know

**Q: What is transfer pricing for a small business with multiple entities?**
A: Transfer pricing describes how much one entity you own charges another for rent, management, staff, or equipment. The IRS expects that price to match what an unrelated party would charge in a comparable deal, even for a small optometry practice or service business with a related real estate LLC.

**Q: Does transfer pricing only apply to large international companies?**
A: No. While transfer pricing is often discussed in a global context, the same arm's length principle applies to any commonly owned entities, including a Bloomington medical practice paying rent or management fees to a related LLC held by the same owner.

**Q: What happens if intercompany pricing isn't documented?**
A: Undocumented pricing between related entities can lead the IRS to reclassify income, disallow deductions tied to inflated fees, and assess penalties and interest. Proper documentation showing the price reflects market terms is the primary defense against these adjustments.
