A client walked into our Bloomington office last spring holding a portfolio statement he hadn't opened in three years. He'd built it right after selling part of his optometry practice: 70% stocks, 30% bonds, diversified across a dozen funds. Smart allocation at the time. By the time he finally looked, tech gains had pushed him to 85% stocks with almost no bond cushion left. He wasn't more aggressive by choice. He was more aggressive by neglect, and he didn't find out until a market pullback cost him more than it should have.

That's the problem with "set it and forget it." Nobody decides to abandon their strategy. The market decides for them, one unmonitored quarter at a time.

## The quiet cost of an unbalanced portfolio

A balanced investment portfolio isn't a one-time construction project. It's closer to a garden: plant it well and walk away, and something is still going to grow out of proportion. Winners take up more space. Losers shrink. Six months later the mix looks nothing like what you designed.

The cost of ignoring this isn't abstract. If your target allocation is 60% equities and 40% fixed income, and equities run hot for two years, you might wake up at 75/25 without touching a single trade. That portfolio carries meaningfully more risk than the one you signed off on. When the correction comes, and it always does eventually, the drawdown hits harder than you planned for, right when you can least afford it: near retirement, mid-way through funding a child's tuition, or during a slow stretch for your practice's revenue.

The Federal Reserve's research on household finance and investment behavior points to a related pattern: many households don't actively rebalance, and inertia itself becomes a risk factor ([Federal Reserve Notes on Household Finance and Investment Behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm)). Doing nothing feels safe. It isn't neutral. It's a decision with consequences, just one nobody signed off on.

## Why allocations drift even when you do nothing

Here's a simple illustration of how fast drift happens without a single new deposit or withdrawal, assuming a starting 60/40 split and a strong run for equities:

| Year | Equity value grows | Bond value grows | Resulting mix |
|------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------|
| Start | $60,000 | $40,000 | 60% / 40% |
| Year 1 (+18% equities, +2% bonds) | $70,800 | $40,800 | 63% / 37% |
| Year 2 (+15% equities, +1% bonds) | $81,420 | $41,208 | 66% / 34% |
| Year 3 (+12% equities, 0% bonds) | $91,190 | $41,208 | 69% / 31% |

No one made a bad decision in that table. The market simply did what markets do. But by year three, this investor is carrying nearly 10 percentage points more equity risk than intended, with zero conscious approval of that shift.

This is why portfolio diversification isn't a set number you pick once. It's a ratio you defend on a schedule, whether that's quarterly, annually, or triggered by a specific percentage of drift.

## Risk tolerance changes even when the market doesn't

Asset allocation strategy isn't only a response to market movement. It's also a response to your life, and your life doesn't hold still either. A physician five years from selling her practice has a different risk tolerance than she did at 40. A service business owner who just took on a business loan to expand has less appetite for a 2008-style drawdown than he did before he had that monthly payment.

We've sat across the table from business owners in Bloomington and Indianapolis who built an allocation around one set of assumptions, then years later were still living under it after a divorce, an inheritance, a practice sale, or a new dependent changed what they could actually afford to lose. Risk tolerance investing has to account for capacity to absorb loss, not just comfort with volatility. Those two things drift apart constantly, and only one of them shows up on a portfolio statement.

## The tax side most investors forget

This is where a CPA's chair looks different from a broker's. Rebalancing a portfolio inside a taxable brokerage account isn't free. Selling appreciated stock to bring your allocation back to target can trigger capital gains, and depending on your income bracket and how long you've held the position, that tax bill can undo a meaningful chunk of the benefit you were chasing.

A few moves change that math substantially:

- Rebalancing inside tax-advantaged accounts first (IRAs, 401(k)s) before touching taxable holdings, since trades there don't create a taxable event.
- Directing new contributions toward the underweighted asset class instead of selling the overweighted one, which rebalances without a sale at all.
- Pairing a rebalancing sale with tax-loss harvesting elsewhere in the same year to offset the gain.
- Timing a larger rebalance around a year with lower ordinary income, such as a sabbatical year or a year following a practice buyout, when your capital gains rate may drop.

None of this is exotic. It's the kind of coordination that happens when your tax preparer and your investment decisions are actually talking to each other, which for most people, they aren't. Jared Hammack, CPA, works through exactly this kind of coordination with individual clients during personal income tax planning, not just at filing time in April but in the months before a rebalancing decision gets made. The AICPA's Personal Financial Planning resources outline the same principle: tax strategy and investment strategy should be built together, not bolted on after the fact ([AICPA Personal Financial Planning Resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning)).

If you're already planning to sell a business, a rental property, or another large asset this year, the rebalancing conversation and the tax conversation need to happen at the same table, not sequentially. ## A simple rebalancing discipline that works

You don't need a complicated system. You need a consistent one. Most individual investors do fine with one of two approaches:

1. **Calendar-based rebalancing.** Review your allocation every year, often paired with year-end tax planning, and rebalance back to target regardless of what moved.
2. **Threshold-based rebalancing.** Set a drift tolerance, commonly 5 percentage points, and rebalance whenever any asset class crosses that line, whatever the calendar says.

Either works. What doesn't work is no system at all, which is what "set it and forget it" actually means in practice. Pair whichever method you choose with your year-end tax review. A balanced portfolio isn't a document you file away. It's a position you actively defend, on a schedule, with tax consequences factored in before you trade, not after.

## Common Questions

**How often should I actually rebalance my portfolio?**
Most individual investors do well rebalancing once a year, ideally tied to year-end tax planning, or whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target. More frequent rebalancing in a taxable account usually creates more tax cost than benefit.

**Does rebalancing always trigger a taxable event?**
Only when you sell appreciated assets in a taxable account. Rebalancing inside an IRA or 401(k) doesn't trigger capital gains, and directing new contributions toward underweighted asset classes can rebalance a taxable account without any sale at all.

**What's the difference between risk tolerance and risk capacity?**
Risk tolerance is your emotional comfort with volatility. Risk capacity is your actual financial ability to absorb a loss without derailing your goals, tied to things like income stability, time horizon, and upcoming obligations. A sound allocation respects both, and both change as your life does.

**Should my CPA be involved in investment decisions, not just my tax return?**
Yes, particularly around rebalancing, asset sales, and any year with an unusual income event like a practice sale or large bonus. Coordinating the tax side of an investment decision before you trade, rather than after, is usually where the real savings show up.
