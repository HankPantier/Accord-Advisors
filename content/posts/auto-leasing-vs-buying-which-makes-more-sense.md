A patient walks into your optometry practice and asks if you're leasing or buying the new visual field analyzer. Nobody asks that question about a car, but they should. Whether you're an optometrist in Bloomington replacing a practice vehicle, a dentist adding a second location and needs reliable transportation between offices, or a self-employed contractor driving to job sites across Monroe County, the lease-versus-buy decision on a vehicle carries the same weight as any other capital purchase. It affects your monthly cash flow, your tax return, and your balance sheet for years.

Most car buyers make this decision based on the monthly payment a dealership quotes. That's backward. The payment is the smallest piece of the puzzle. The real answer depends on how many miles you drive, whether the vehicle is used for business, how long you typically keep a car, and what your tax situation looks like this year versus five years from now.

## The real cost comparison dealerships won't show you

A lease payment is almost always lower than a loan payment on the same vehicle. That's by design. You're financing the depreciation during the lease term, not the full purchase price. A $45,000 SUV might lease for $550 a month over 36 months but cost $780 a month on a 60-month loan. The lease looks cheaper until you calculate what you actually own at the end.

After three years of leasing, you have nothing. After five years of a loan, you own a vehicle that still has resale or trade-in value, often $15,000 to $20,000 depending on mileage and condition. Add up total payments plus mileage penalties (typically 15 to 25 cents per mile over the lease limit) and buying frequently wins on pure dollar cost if you plan to keep the vehicle past the loan term.

Interest rates change this math every year. The Federal Reserve's [Consumer Credit (G.19) report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) tracks auto loan rate trends, and when rates climb, the gap between lease and loan payments narrows because both are financing costs. Before you sign anything, it's worth checking where rates currently sit rather than assuming the numbers from two years ago still apply.

## How Section 179 changes the math for business vehicles

If the vehicle is used for business, purchasing opens a door leasing does not: accelerated depreciation. Under Section 179 of the tax code, business owners can deduct a significant portion of a vehicle's cost in the year it's placed in service, rather than depreciating it over five or six years. The rules distinguish between passenger vehicles (capped deduction amounts) and heavier vehicles like certain SUVs and trucks over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight, which qualify for a much larger first-year deduction.

The [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the specific limits, business-use percentage requirements, and how bonus depreciation stacks on top of Section 179 for qualifying vehicles. A dentist buying a used truck to haul equipment between two Bloomington offices, at 80% business use, could see a first-year deduction that meaningfully offsets that year's taxable income. A leased vehicle only lets you deduct the business-use portion of the lease payment itself, spread evenly across the term. There's no acceleration, no lump-sum benefit.

This is where a lot of business owners get bad advice from a salesperson instead of a CPA. A dealership's finance manager isn't calculating your effective tax rate or your Schedule C business-use percentage. Jared Hammack, CPA, works through this exact calculation with optometry and medical practice clients every year, because the right answer depends on your specific taxable income, not a rule of thumb a friend mentioned at dinner.

## Cash flow and driving habits matter more than the sticker price

Before any tax discussion, ask three questions:

1. How many miles do you drive in a typical year? Most leases cap you at 10,000 to 12,000 miles. A service-business owner covering a multi-county territory around Bloomington and Indianapolis will blow past that limit and pay $0.15 to $0.25 per extra mile at lease-end, which adds up fast on a 15,000-mile-a-year driver.
2. How long do you keep vehicles? If you trade in every three years regardless, leasing avoids the depreciation hit and keeps you in a newer, lower-maintenance vehicle. If you drive cars into the ground, buying wins outright.
3. What does your monthly cash flow look like right now? A practice in its first two years of operation, still building reserves, might prioritize the lower lease payment to preserve cash for payroll and equipment. A mature practice with steady revenue can absorb the higher loan payment and capture the ownership equity.

There's no universal right answer here. A solo optometrist just opening a second location has a different cash position than an established family medicine practice with fifteen years of consistent revenue.

## When leasing makes sense for a Bloomington practice owner

Leasing tends to fit situations where the vehicle is a tool, not an asset you want to hold. If you're a service-based business owner who drives a branded van to client sites and wants the newest safety technology every few years, leasing keeps maintenance costs low and the vehicle under warranty. If your business use is under 50%, the tax advantages of purchasing shrink considerably, since Section 179 deductions require business use above that threshold to claim the full benefit. And if preserving cash for payroll, inventory, or a practice buildout matters more this year than building long-term vehicle equity, the lower lease payment buys you breathing room.

Leasing also makes sense for anyone who dreads the trade-in negotiation. You hand the keys back, walk into a new lease, and never touch the used-car market.

## When buying builds more long-term value

Buying wins when you plan to keep the vehicle five years or more, when your annual mileage regularly exceeds lease limits, or when your business income is high enough this year that the Section 179 deduction produces real tax savings. It also wins for anyone who values owning an asset outright rather than making payments indefinitely. Once the loan is paid off, that vehicle either keeps working for you at no monthly cost or converts to cash at trade-in.

For a solo dentist or optometrist who bought a first practice vehicle in year two and is now generating consistent profit, purchasing a used truck or SUV, applying Section 179, and holding it for six or seven years often produces a better after-tax outcome than a rolling series of leases.

## A simple framework to decide

| Factor | Favors leasing | Favors buying |
|---|---|---|
| Annual mileage | Under 12,000 | Over 15,000 |
| Ownership horizon | Under 4 years | 5+ years |
| Business-use percentage | Under 50% | Over 50% |
| Current cash position | Tight, building reserves | Stable, consistent profit |
| Tax goal | Steady, predictable deduction | Large first-year deduction |

Run your own numbers against this table before you sit down with a dealership finance manager. Better yet, run them with a CPA who already knows your business-use percentage and your practice's tax bracket, because that context changes which column wins.

## Common Questions

**Is leasing or buying better for tax purposes?**
Buying generally offers a larger tax benefit for business vehicles because of Section 179 and bonus depreciation, which allow a significant first-year deduction on qualifying vehicles over 6,000 pounds. Leasing only allows you to deduct the business-use percentage of the monthly payment, spread evenly across the lease term, with no acceleration.

**Can I deduct a leased car for business use?**
Yes. You can deduct the business-use percentage of your lease payments, along with gas, insurance, and maintenance tied to business use, but you cannot claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation on a leased vehicle since you don't own it.

**Does Section 179 apply to personal vehicles?**
Section 179 only applies to vehicles used more than 50% for business. A car used mostly for commuting and personal errands, even if occasionally used for work, generally won't qualify for the full deduction. [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) outlines the business-use threshold and documentation requirements in detail.

**How do interest rates affect the lease vs. buy decision?**
Higher rates raise both loan and lease costs, but they tend to hit loan payments harder since you're financing the full purchase price rather than just the depreciation. Checking current auto loan trends through the [Federal Reserve's G.19 report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/) before signing a multi-year agreement is a reasonable step for any business owner financing a vehicle.
