## Why churches and nonprofits are common fraud targets

Nonprofits and churches run lean. A single bookkeeper often handles deposits, disbursements, and reconciliations because the budget doesn't support a full accounting team. That concentration of duties, combined with a culture built on trust rather than suspicion, creates exactly the conditions fraud examiners warn about.

The Association of Certified Fraud Examiners has found nonprofit organizations lose a median of $75,000 per fraud case, often absorbed by an organization that can least afford it. Volunteers handle cash offerings on Sunday mornings. Small staffs mean the same person who opens the mail also records the deposit and reconciles the bank statement. Board members, often volunteers themselves, may not have the accounting background needed to spot irregularities in a financial statement.

Bloomington and Indianapolis-area nonprofits face this reality without the internal audit departments larger organizations rely on. That's where practical, right-sized controls make the difference.

## Warning signs of internal fraud in nonprofit organizations

Fraud rarely announces itself. It shows up in small inconsistencies that get explained away until they can't be anymore. Board members and church leaders in Bloomington should treat these signs as reasons to look closer, not as one-off mistakes.

- Bank or credit card statements that go unreconciled for more than a month
- Missing receipts, invoices, or documentation for reimbursements
- One person controlling deposits, disbursements, and recordkeeping with no second signature
- Vendors or payees the board doesn't recognize
- A staff member or volunteer reluctant to take vacation or hand off duties
- Financial reports that arrive late, inconsistent, or without detail
- Donor or contribution records that don't match bank deposits

Any one of these might have an innocent explanation. Several together, especially around cash handling or reconciliations, warrant a closer look from someone outside day-to-day operations.

## Core internal controls every nonprofit should have

Fraud prevention isn't about distrust. It's about structure. These four controls form the foundation of [Business Foundation Services](/services/business-foundation-services) for churches and nonprofits, and they cost far less to put in place than a single embezzlement case costs to absorb.

### Segregation of duties
icon: Users

No single person should open mail, make deposits, record transactions, and reconcile the bank statement. Splitting these tasks across two or more people, even in a five-person office, closes the gap most embezzlement schemes exploit.

### Dual approval on payments
icon: ShieldCheck

Checks and electronic payments above a set threshold require a second signature or approval. This one step stops the majority of check-tampering and unauthorized disbursement schemes before money leaves the account.

### Monthly reconciliations
icon: FileCheck

Bank and credit card statements get reconciled every month, not whenever there's time. Discrepancies get caught within weeks instead of surfacing a year later during an audit.

### Board-level financial oversight
icon: ClipboardCheck

Someone outside daily operations, typically a treasurer or finance committee, reviews financial statements each month and asks questions about anything that doesn't look right.

## How outsourced accounting strengthens fraud protection

Handing bookkeeping to one internal employee, however trusted, still leaves a single point of failure. Accord Advisors' [outsourced accounting](/services/outsourced-accounting) model splits the work across a team: one person handles data entry, another reviews and reconciles, and a CPA reviews the financials before they reach the board. That separation exists automatically, without hiring additional staff.

Every transaction runs through a secure online platform where church and nonprofit leaders can see activity in real time rather than waiting for a year-end audit to find a problem. Fixed monthly fees mean there's no incentive to skip reconciliations or shortcut a review to save billable hours, a risk that comes built into hourly-billed engagements.

Jared Hammack, CPA, has spent close to 40 years working with healthcare providers, service businesses, and nonprofit organizations across Bloomington and Indianapolis, and has seen firsthand how thin staffing turns good intentions into open doors for fraud. Outsourcing the accounting function doesn't just save time. It rebuilds the checks and balances a two-person office can't provide on its own.

## Financial dashboards and reporting that catch problems early

Boards and church councils can't act on numbers they don't see. Accord Advisors provides monthly financial reporting built around the questions leadership actually asks: how did the organization perform this month, are expenses tracking where expected, and does contribution income match what came in through the offering plate or online giving platform.

Dashboards flag variances as they happen rather than months later. A vendor payment that doesn't match a prior pattern, a reimbursement that spikes unexpectedly, or a reconciliation that won't balance shows up in the reporting a board member reviews each month, not in a forensic accountant's report after the money is gone.

That kind of visibility does double duty. It supports fraud prevention, and it gives boards the operational insight they need to make informed decisions about programs, staffing, and giving trends throughout the year.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Fraud prevention strategies for nonprofit organizations

**Q: What are the most common types of fraud in nonprofit organizations?**
A: Check tampering, skimming from cash donations, and payroll fraud are the most frequent schemes reported in nonprofit fraud studies. Small organizations with one person handling deposits and reconciliations face the highest risk because no second set of eyes reviews transactions before they clear the bank.

**Q: How much does nonprofit fraud typically cost an organization?**
A: Fraud examiners report a median loss of $75,000 per case at nonprofit organizations, though losses can run far higher when schemes go undetected for years. For a nonprofit or church operating on donor contributions, that kind of loss can mean cutting programs or staff.
