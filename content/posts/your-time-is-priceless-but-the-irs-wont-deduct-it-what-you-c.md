If you drove to the food pantry every Saturday this year, bought supplies for a church mission trip, or spent your evenings keeping the books for a nonprofit board, you gave something real. The IRS agrees that your generosity has value, just not in the way most volunteers assume. Your time and labor, no matter how skilled or how many hours you logged, are worth zero dollars on a tax return. But the money you spent out of pocket to make that volunteering possible? That's often deductible, and most people leave it on the table because they never learned the rules.

This matters more in Bloomington and Indianapolis than people realize. Between IU-affiliated volunteer programs, church mission trips, and the boards that keep local nonprofits running, plenty of taxpayers here rack up real unreimbursed costs every year. Knowing what counts, and documenting it correctly, can turn a few hundred dollars of generosity into a few hundred dollars of tax savings.

## Why your volunteer hours have no dollar value to the IRS

The rule is blunt: you cannot deduct the value of your time or services, no matter what your normal hourly rate is. A dentist who volunteers a Saturday doing free cleanings at a community clinic cannot deduct what those services would have billed at. A CPA who does the books for a church for free cannot deduct her standard hourly rate either. The IRS treats donated labor and donated time as contributions with no measurable value for deduction purposes, full stop.

What changes the math is money you actually spend from your own pocket in the course of that volunteer work, for a qualified organization, that you're not reimbursed for. That's the piece most people misunderstand. They either think none of their costs count because "it's just volunteering," or they think their time itself has some deductible worth. Both assumptions cost people money, one way or the other. [IRS Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) lays out exactly where the line falls, and it's worth five minutes of reading before you toss your receipts.

## The charitable mileage deduction most volunteers forget

Driving counts. If you use your own vehicle to get to and from volunteer work for a qualified organization, you can deduct 14 cents per mile, a rate set by statute rather than adjusted annually like the business mileage rate. Alternatively, you can deduct your actual out-of-pocket costs for gas and oil directly tied to the trips, though not depreciation, insurance, or general maintenance.

A few concrete examples make this easier to picture:

- Driving from Bloomington to deliver meals for a church outreach program twice a week
- Hauling donated furniture to a nonprofit thrift store in Indianapolis
- Commuting to serve on a hospital or clinic volunteer rotation
- Traveling to attend required training sessions for a volunteer role

Parking fees and tolls paid during these trips are deductible on top of the mileage, whichever method you choose. Keep a simple log: date, destination, purpose, and miles driven. A notes app or a small notebook in the glovebox is enough. Without that record, the deduction won't survive a closer look if your return is ever questioned.

## Out-of-pocket costs that qualify beyond mileage

Mileage gets the attention, but it's often not the biggest number on the list. Unreimbursed volunteer expenses can include:

- Supplies you buy and donate directly for a project, like paint for a church renovation or craft materials for a youth program
- Uniforms required by the organization that aren't suitable for everyday wear, plus the cost of cleaning them
- Reasonable travel costs for volunteer trips away from home, including lodging and half the cost of meals, when there's no significant element of personal pleasure or vacation involved
- Phone calls made specifically for the organization's work
- Costs of hosting a fundraising event on behalf of a qualified charity, such as renting chairs or printing materials, when you're not reimbursed

The common thread is that the expense has to be directly connected to the volunteer work, unreimbursed, and paid to or on behalf of an organization that qualifies under IRS rules. A quick check of an organization's status is available through the [IRS Charities & Nonprofits resource hub](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits) before you assume a donation or expense qualifies.

## What still doesn't count, even if it feels like it should

This is where good intentions run into hard limits. The value of your labor, professional skills, or time never counts, regardless of your normal billing rate. Childcare costs you pay so you can go volunteer are personal expenses, not charitable ones. Your own meals during a single day of local volunteering aren't deductible either, only away-from-home travel meals qualify, and only at half the cost.

Here's a simple comparison of what typically clears the bar and what doesn't:

| Deductible | Not deductible |
|---|---|
| Mileage to and from volunteer shifts | The market value of hours worked |
| Supplies bought and donated for a project | Childcare while you volunteer |
| Required uniforms and cleaning costs | Everyday clothing worn while volunteering |
| Travel and lodging for an out-of-town service trip | Meals eaten during local, same-day volunteering |
| Long-distance calls made for the organization | A professional's normal hourly rate for donated services |

One more wrinkle worth knowing: if any part of a volunteer trip involves significant personal vacation time, the IRS expects you to allocate costs and only deduct the portion tied to actual volunteer work.

## Records the IRS expects before you claim anything

The deduction rules are generous compared to what most volunteers assume, but they come with a documentation requirement that a lot of taxpayers skip. For any single contribution or expense of $250 or more, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization describing the services you provided and confirming you received no goods or services in return. For mileage and smaller out-of-pocket costs, a dated log and receipts are enough, but they need to exist before you file, not reconstructed from memory in April.

A reasonable system looks like this: keep a running log of volunteer mileage with dates and purpose, save receipts for anything you buy on behalf of the organization, and ask for a letter from the charity at year-end summarizing your volunteer role if the total unreimbursed cost is likely to cross $250. Board members and church volunteers who handle money or make regular purchases for the organization should treat this as routine, not an annual scramble. If you also sit on a nonprofit board or handle church finances, it's worth reviewing our [fraud prevention strategies for nonprofit organizations](/resources/fraud-prevention-strategies-for-nonprofit-organizations) alongside your recordkeeping, since the same internal controls that protect an organization also make your own deduction records cleaner. Churches with staff or clergy on payroll should also look at how [staffing and compliance rules](/industries/churches/staffing-and-compliance) intersect with volunteer reimbursement policies, since the two areas overlap more than most treasurers expect.

If this is starting to sound like the same lesson as other narrow IRS categories, it is. The rules around what counts as a legitimate deduction versus a personal expense show up constantly, from [dog-related business deductions](/resources/unless-its-working-for-you-your-dog-isnt-a-tax-deduction) to volunteer mileage. The IRS draws specific lines, and the taxpayers who benefit are the ones who know exactly where those lines sit before they file.

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct the value of professional services I donate, like legal or accounting work?**
No. The IRS specifically disallows a deduction for the value of your time, skills, or services, even if you would normally bill for that work at a professional rate. Only unreimbursed, out-of-pocket costs connected to the volunteer work are deductible.

**What's the current charitable mileage rate for volunteer driving?**
The rate is 14 cents per mile for miles driven in service of a qualified charitable organization. This rate is set by Congress and does not adjust annually the way the standard business mileage rate does, so it's worth checking current guidance before you calculate your total.

**Do I need a receipt for every small volunteer expense?**
For anything under $250, a dated log or basic receipt is generally sufficient. Once a single contribution of money or unreimbursed expenses reaches $250, you need a written acknowledgment from the organization describing what you provided and confirming you received nothing of value in return.

**Can I deduct travel costs for a mission trip if I also spend a few days sightseeing?**
Only the portion of the trip genuinely devoted to volunteer work is deductible. If a significant part of the trip involves personal vacation time, you'll need to allocate travel and lodging costs between the charitable and personal portions.
