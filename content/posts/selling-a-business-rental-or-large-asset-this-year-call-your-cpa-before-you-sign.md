## Why timing your call matters more than the sale price

Most of the real tax planning on a sale happens before you sign anything, not after. Installment sale elections, cost segregation reviews, entity restructuring, and gain deferral strategies all depend on decisions made while the deal is still a draft. Once ink hits paper, the IRS treats the transaction as done, and so do most of the planning options that would have reduced your tax bill.

We see this most often with optometrists selling a practice, dentists retiring out of a group, service business owners entertaining a buyout offer, and landlords negotiating the sale of a rental property in Bloomington or Indianapolis. The sale price matters. What matters more is what you keep after taxes, and that number gets decided weeks or months before closing, not at the closing table.

A short conversation with a CPA before you sign can change the structure of the deal itself, not just the paperwork around it.

## Common transactions that need advance tax planning

Every sale looks different depending on the entity, the asset, and the buyer. A few scenarios come up often with our clients:

- An optometrist selling a practice to an associate or a group, where goodwill allocation and non-compete payments change the tax treatment of nearly every dollar
- A dentist retiring and transferring equipment, chairs, and instruments, where depreciation recapture can turn a routine handoff into a surprise tax bill
- A service business owner exiting through an asset sale or stock sale, where the choice between the two changes who pays what and when
- A landlord selling a rental building, where depreciation recapture and 1031 exchange timelines have hard deadlines that do not bend
- A church disposing of property, where unrelated business income rules and use restrictions require a different kind of review entirely

Each of these needs its own strategy. None of them benefit from a generic answer worked out after the fact.

<!-- block: content-prose -->
## What can go wrong when you sign first and call later

The damage from skipping pre-sale planning is rarely dramatic. It shows up quietly, as a bigger tax bill than expected or a planning option that closed the moment the contract was executed.

An installment sale election, for example, has to be built into how the deal is structured, not applied afterward. Miss that window and a seller who could have spread a gain over several years owes it all in one. Depreciation recapture on equipment or real estate is calculated based on what was claimed over the years the asset was owned, and there is no way to undo that exposure once the sale closes. Capital gains treatment can shift dramatically depending on how a purchase agreement allocates the price between goodwill, equipment, and real property, a detail buyers and sellers often negotiate without anyone flagging the tax consequence.

None of this means a deal is doomed if you have not called us yet. It means the earlier the call happens, the more options are still on the table.

## What to bring to your pre-sale consultation

The first meeting goes faster, and further, when you bring the numbers with you. For most sellers, that means:

- Any draft purchase agreement or letter of intent, even an early one
- Current depreciation schedules for the business, equipment, or property involved
- Original purchase price and improvements made to the asset since acquisition
- Recent financial statements or tax returns for the business or rental activity
- A rough timeline for the sale, including any deadlines the buyer has proposed

You do not need a finished deal or a signed contract to have this conversation. A term sheet, a verbal offer, or even a serious inquiry from a buyer is enough to start the planning work.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Selling a business rental or large asset this year call your CPA before you sign

**Q: When should I call a CPA before selling my business or rental property?**
A: As soon as you start negotiating, even before a letter of intent is signed. Tax strategies like installment sale elections and entity restructuring have to be built into the deal structure itself, and most of those options disappear once a contract is executed.

**Q: What tax planning options disappear after I sign a sale agreement?**
A: Installment sale elections, purchase price allocation between goodwill and equipment, and certain gain deferral strategies typically must be negotiated before signing. Depreciation recapture on real estate or equipment also becomes fixed based on the sale terms, leaving no room to restructure afterward.

**Q: Does a rental property sale need the same tax planning as a business sale?**
A: No. Rental property sales involve depreciation recapture and possible 1031 exchange deadlines, while business sales involve goodwill allocation, entity structure, and sometimes non-compete payments. Each transaction type needs its own review well before closing.

**Q: What documents should I bring to a pre-sale tax planning meeting?**
A: Bring any draft purchase agreement or letter of intent, current depreciation schedules, the original purchase price and improvement history, and recent financial statements or tax returns. Even an early draft or verbal offer is enough to begin the conversation.
