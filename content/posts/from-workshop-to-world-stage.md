## A Bloomington business built on craftsmanship

Ten years ago, a custom furniture maker on Bloomington's west side worked out of a two-car garage, building dining tables and cabinetry one order at a time. Word spread. A feature in a regional design magazine led to a wholesale order from a Midwest retailer, then a call from a buyer in Indianapolis, then requests from as far as the West Coast. Within three years, the shop had outgrown the garage and moved into a proper workspace with four employees and a waitlist.

Growth like that is the goal, but it comes with a problem most craftsmen never planned for: the books that worked fine at $80,000 a year in revenue fall apart at $800,000. Spreadsheets get messy. Nobody has time to reconcile a bank account between orders. The owner knew wood and finish work cold. Payroll tax deposits and job costing were another matter entirely.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image-left | image: owner-reviewing-financial-paperwork.jpg | alt: "Small business owner reviewing paper financial statements at a workbench" | query: "small business owner reviewing paperwork" -->
## The financial blind spots that come with growth

By year four, the shop was profitable on paper but the owner couldn't say with confidence how the last month had actually performed. That's a common gap for service and production businesses scaling past their founding structure, and it usually shows up in the same places:

- No reliable monthly numbers, just a bank balance and a hunch
- Personal and business expenses tangled together in one account
- Job costs estimated instead of tracked, so pricing decisions were guesswork
- No dashboard showing which product lines or clients actually drove profit
- Payroll and sales tax handled reactively, close to deadlines instead of ahead of them
- No clear picture of cash flow heading into a big wholesale order

The business was growing faster than its financial systems could keep up with. That gap, left alone, tends to catch up with owners right when they can least afford it: during a big order, a loan application, or a slow season nobody saw coming.

## Building a financial foundation that could scale

This is where Business Foundation Services came in. Before monthly numbers mean anything, the underlying structure has to be right: a chart of accounts that matches how the business actually operates, a clean separation between personal and business finances, and an entity structure built for the growth already underway rather than the one-person shop from a decade earlier.

Accord Advisors rebuilt the books from the ground up, set up job costing so every wholesale order had a real margin attached to it, and put reporting systems in place that could keep pace as the business expanded from Bloomington toward Indianapolis and, eventually, national accounts. The foundation work took a few months. It's the difference between a business that can only look backward at what happened and one that can see what's coming.

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-right | image: laptop-financial-dashboard-review.jpg | alt: "Business owner viewing monthly financial dashboard on a laptop screen" | query: "laptop financial dashboard review" -->
## Outsourced accounting that freed up time to create

With the foundation set, the shop moved to ongoing outsourced accounting on a fixed monthly fee, no hourly clock running in the background. Every month, the owner logs into a secure online platform and sees exactly how the business performed: revenue by product line, job margins, cash position, payroll costs, all in one place.

That clarity replaced guesswork. Instead of estimating whether a big order was worth taking, the owner could check actual margins from the last three similar jobs before quoting. Instead of hiring a full-time controller, a role most shops this size can't yet justify, the business gets CFO-level visibility at a fraction of that cost. The time that used to go toward reconciling accounts now goes toward the workshop floor, where the actual craft happens.
