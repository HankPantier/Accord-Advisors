A knee replacement is manageable. A stroke that leaves someone unable to dress or bathe without help is a different problem entirely, and it's the one most families never plan for. Genworth's Cost of Care data has long shown nursing home care running well above $90,000 a year nationally, and Indiana isn't far behind that number for a private room. Medicare doesn't cover it. Health insurance doesn't cover it. That leaves three real options: insurance, veterans benefits, and your own savings, used in some combination decided years before anyone needs a wheelchair ramp.

This is a financial-risk decision, not an emotional one, even though it feels like both. The families who handle it well are the ones who treat long-term care the way they treat disability insurance or a business succession plan: fund it early, review it regularly, and don't wait for a diagnosis to start the conversation.

## What long-term care costs a family in central Indiana

Genworth's most recent survey puts the median annual cost of a private nursing home room in Indiana in the neighborhood of $100,000, with assisted living facilities running $55,000 to $65,000 and in-home health aides billing by the hour, often adding up to $60,000 or more a year for full-time care. Those numbers rise every year, typically 3% to 5%, which means a couple in their late 50s planning for care in their 80s should expect costs roughly double today's figures by the time they need them.

Most people underestimate this by a wide margin. They picture a few months of rehab after surgery. The reality for a lot of families is multiple years of care, sometimes five or more, and the bill doesn't pause because savings ran out.

## Why waiting for a crisis costs more than planning ahead

Long-term care insurance has medical underwriting. Wait until a Parkinson's diagnosis or a fall that breaks a hip, and you're no longer eligible to buy a policy at any price. Self-funding works only if the money has time to grow and if you've actually set it aside in an account earmarked for this purpose, not folded into general retirement savings that are already committed to daily living expenses. VA Aid and Attendance benefits require proof of wartime service and an application process that can take months. None of these three tools work well as an emergency response. All three work well as a plan.

The planning window that matters most is age 55 to 65. Insurance premiums are lower, health qualifies more easily, and there's enough runway left for invested savings to compound before care is needed. Clients who bring this up at 78, usually after a parent's care crisis makes it personal, have far fewer good options left.

## Comparing the three ways to pay for long-term care

| Funding method | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Healthy individuals age 50-65 with assets to protect | Premiums can rise; buy from a financially strong carrier |
| VA Aid and Attendance | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses with modest income and assets | Application takes months; net worth limits apply |
| Self-funding | High net worth or those who don't qualify for insurance or VA benefits | Requires dedicated, invested savings, not just "having money" |

Most families end up blending two of these, and sometimes all three, rather than betting everything on one.

## Long-term care insurance versus self-funding

A standalone long-term care policy purchased at 58 might run $2,500 to $4,000 a year for $200,000 to $300,000 of lifetime benefit, depending on health and the inflation rider selected. That premium is a known, budgetable cost. Compare it to self-funding the same benefit: at a 6% return, it takes disciplined annual savings of roughly $6,000 to $8,000 over 15 years to build an equivalent pool, and that money has to stay invested and untouched, which is harder than it sounds when a roof needs replacing or a grandchild needs tuition help.

Self-funding makes the most sense for business owners and practice owners who already have $2 million or more in investable assets and can absorb a bad year of care costs without restructuring their retirement plan. It makes less sense for someone whose net worth is tied up in a home, a practice, or illiquid real estate. If your investment portfolio is built to fund retirement income first, adding a long-term care liability on top without rebalancing for it is a common mistake. Our piece on [keeping a balanced investment portfolio properly rebalanced](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) covers the mechanics of adjusting a portfolio as goals shift, which applies directly here.

Hybrid life insurance policies with long-term care riders have become popular because they solve the "use it or lose it" objection: if care is never needed, the death benefit still pays out to heirs. They typically cost more upfront but remove the risk of paying premiums for decades and never filing a claim.

## VA Aid and Attendance benefits for veterans and surviving spouses

Veterans who served during a wartime period, and surviving spouses who haven't remarried, may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit, an increased pension amount for those who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medication. As of recent VA guidance, the benefit can add over $2,300 a month for a married veteran or roughly $1,500 for a surviving spouse, on top of base pension, which meaningfully offsets the cost of in-home care or assisted living. Full details on eligibility, net worth limits, and the application process are on the [VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

This benefit gets missed constantly. Families assume VA benefits mean disability compensation for combat injuries, and they don't realize a healthy veteran who later develops dementia at 82 can still qualify based on current care needs, not service-connected injury. If you or a parent served, this is worth checking before assuming self-funding is the only route.

## Fitting long-term care into a broader financial and tax plan

Long-term care insurance premiums are often deductible as a medical expense, subject to age-based limits and the 7.5% adjusted gross income threshold, and qualified long-term care services themselves may also qualify. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) lays out exactly which long-term care premiums and services count as deductible medical expenses, including the age-based premium caps that increase as you get older. That's a detail worth reviewing with a CPA each year, since the deductible premium limit changes annually and most people never check it.

For business owners, there's an added angle: a properly structured C-corporation can deduct 100% of long-term care insurance premiums for owners and employees as a business expense, a strategy that's often overlooked because it sits at the intersection of tax planning and estate planning, two areas that don't always get discussed together. ## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care insurance cost at age 60?**
A healthy 60-year-old typically pays $2,800 to $4,500 a year for a policy with $200,000 to $300,000 of lifetime benefit and a 3% inflation rider. Premiums vary based on health history, gender, and whether you buy an individual or shared-care policy with a spouse.

**Can I get VA Aid and Attendance benefits and long-term care insurance at the same time?**
Yes. VA benefits and private long-term care insurance aren't mutually exclusive, and combining them often covers a larger share of in-home or facility care costs than either would alone. The VA benefit is based on net worth and income limits, not on whether you carry other insurance.

**Is self-funding long-term care ever the better choice?**
It can be, particularly for business owners and high-net-worth individuals with $2 million or more in investable assets who can absorb care costs without disrupting retirement income. The tradeoff is that the money has to be genuinely set aside and invested for this purpose, not counted twice in a retirement plan.

**Does long-term care insurance count as a tax deduction?**
Often, yes. Premiums are deductible as a medical expense up to age-based limits set by the IRS, and C-corporations can deduct premiums for owners and employees as a business expense in many structures. [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502) has the current premium limits by age.
