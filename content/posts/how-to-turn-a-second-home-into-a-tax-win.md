A cabin at Lake Monroe or a condo near the Gulf Coast can be more than a place to unwind. Depending on how many days you use it yourself and how many days you rent it out, the IRS will slot that property into one of three very different tax categories, and each one comes with its own rules for deductions, depreciation, and reported income. Buy first and figure out the tax treatment later, and you can end up losing deductions you didn't know you were entitled to, or worse, owing tax on income you thought was tax-free.

The good news: the rules are not vague. IRS Publication 527 and Tax Topic 415 lay out a bright-line test based on personal-use days versus rental days, and once you know which side of that line your property falls on, planning around it is straightforward.

## The 14-day rule that makes rental income disappear

Here's the rule most vacation home owners have never heard of. If you rent out your second home for 14 days or fewer during the year, you don't report the rental income at all, no matter how much you collected. Rent your Brown County cabin out for a single weekend during the fall foliage rush for $2,800 and that money is yours, tax-free, as long as you stayed under the 14-day threshold for the full year.

The trade-off is that you still treat the property as a personal residence. Mortgage interest and property taxes are deductible the normal way, on Schedule A, subject to the usual itemized deduction limits. You don't get to deduct cleaning fees, a property manager's commission, or depreciation, because the IRS isn't treating this as a rental business. For an owner who rents out a property occasionally to offset carrying costs, this is often the best outcome available, and it requires zero extra paperwork beyond tracking your rental days.

## What happens once you cross 15 rental days

Cross the 15-day threshold and the property becomes a rental for tax purposes, but which set of rules applies still depends on your personal use. The IRS measures personal use against the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days the home was actually rented at fair value. Days spent doing repairs and maintenance don't count as personal use, but time your family spends there, even a discounted week for a cousin, does.

| Rental days per year | Personal use days | IRS classification | Deduction treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14 or fewer | Any amount | Personal residence, rental income excluded | Mortgage interest and property taxes only, on Schedule A |
| 15 or more | More than 14 days (or more than 10% of rental days) | Personal residence with rental use | Expenses split between personal and rental use; rental losses limited to rental income, excess carried forward |
| 15 or more | 14 days or fewer (and 10% or less of rental days) | Rental/investment property | Full depreciation and expense deductions; losses subject to passive activity limits |

If your personal use stays light, say two weeks a year at a lake house you rent out 200 nights through a booking platform, the property is treated as a true rental. You can depreciate it, deduct property management fees, insurance, utilities, and repairs in full, and if the numbers show a loss, you may be able to use it against other income, subject to passive activity rules under [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

If you and your family use it more than that ceiling, the IRS still calls it a residence with rental use. You allocate expenses between personal and rental days, and any rental loss is capped at your rental income for the year. You don't lose the excess forever, it carries forward to future years, but you can't use it to offset your W-2 or practice income today.

## Second home versus investment property is not just semantics

Lenders, insurers, and the IRS all draw a line between a "second home" and an "investment property," and the distinction affects more than your interest rate. A second home is one you or your family use for a meaningful part of the year. An investment property is one held primarily to generate rental income, with little or no personal use.

That distinction changes how mortgage interest gets deducted. On a qualified second home, mortgage interest is deductible on Schedule A, combined with your primary residence up to the $750,000 acquisition debt limit. On a straight investment property, mortgage interest is instead deducted as a rental expense against rental income, with no cap tied to the $750,000 threshold. Depreciation is only available once the property crosses into rental-property territory, not while it's classified as a personal residence with occasional rental use.

This is where a lot of well-intentioned owners get tripped up. They buy a property planning to rent it heavily, use it themselves more than they expected, and end up with a hybrid tax picture they never modeled for. ## Building a record that survives an audit

The personal-use versus rental-day test only works if you can document both sides. Keep a simple log: dates rented, to whom, and at what rate, alongside dates you or family members used the property personally. If you rent through a platform that issues a 1099-K, reconcile that form against your own log every year, because the IRS will.

Expenses need the same discipline. Mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs, HOA dues, and management fees should all be tracked separately from your other accounts, ideally through a dedicated account or a bookkeeping system built for the property. Clients who treat a vacation rental like a small business from day one, rather than an afterthought at tax time, consistently end up with cleaner returns and fewer surprises.

## Common Questions

**Does renting my second home for a week during a local event count against the 14-day rule**?Yes. Any day you rent the property at fair market value counts toward your rental-day total, and any day you use it personally, or let family use it, counts toward your personal-use total. A single rented week during, say, the Little 500 in Bloomington counts the same as a full month rented in Florida when the IRS runs the math.

**Can I deduct a loss on my second home if I use it myself part of the year**?It depends on how much you use it. If your personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of the days rented, whichever is greater, your rental loss deduction is capped at your rental income for the year, with the excess carried forward under [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415). If personal use stays below that threshold, the property is treated as a rental, and losses may be deductible against other income subject to passive activity rules.

**Is a second home mortgage deducted the same way as a rental property mortgage**?No. A qualified second home's mortgage interest is deducted on Schedule A along with your primary residence, up to the combined $750,000 acquisition debt limit. A property classified as a rental deducts mortgage interest as a business expense against rental income instead, with different limits and reporting.

**What records do I need to keep to prove my rental and personal use days**?A simple log showing dates rented, renter names or platform, rate charged, and dates of personal use is enough, as long as you keep it consistently and reconcile it against any 1099-K or booking platform statement you receive each year.
