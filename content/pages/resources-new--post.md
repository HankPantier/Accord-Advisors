## Why this topic matters to local business owners

Every optometry practice, dental office, and service business we work with in Bloomington and Indianapolis asks a version of the same question at some point: how did we actually do last month? Not gut feeling, not a bank balance glance, but real numbers that show whether the practice grew, stalled, or quietly bled cash on supplies and payroll.

Owners running a Monroe County practice or a growing service business in Indianapolis rarely have time to build that clarity themselves. Between patient care, staffing, and the daily fires that come with running a business, the monthly dashboard gets skipped. That gap is where good decisions turn into guesses, and it's the exact gap this post is built to close.

<!-- block: content-split | variant: image-right | image: financial-dashboard-review.jpg | alt: "Business owner reviewing monthly financial dashboard on laptop screen" | query: "business owner reviewing financial report laptop" -->
## What the numbers are telling you

A monthly financial dashboard is a short, plain-language snapshot: revenue against last month and last year, gross margin by service line, cash on hand, days in accounts receivable, and payroll as a percentage of revenue. For an optometry practice, that might mean tracking exam volume against eyewear sales. For a service business, it might mean labor cost per job.

The point isn't the spreadsheet. It's the pattern. A dip in gross margin two months running usually means supply costs crept up or pricing hasn't kept pace with inflation. A steady rise in accounts receivable days often signals a billing or collections problem before it shows up as a cash crunch.

Most owners we talk to in Bloomington have some of this data buried in their bookkeeping software. Almost none of them have it organized into something they can read in five minutes and act on. That's the difference between having numbers and having answers, and it's why dashboards matter more than raw reports.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image | image: reviewing-practice-financials.jpg | alt: "Practice manager and accountant reviewing printed financial statements together" | query: "accountant client reviewing financial statements" -->
## Common mistakes practices and businesses make

We see the same handful of missteps across optometry, dental, medical, service business, and church clients:

- **Reviewing financials once a quarter, or once a year at tax time.** By the time a problem shows up in the annual numbers, three or four months of decisions have already been made on bad information.
- **Tracking revenue without tracking margin.** An optometry practice can grow top-line revenue while gross margin quietly shrinks because frame costs or lab fees rose without a corresponding fee adjustment.
- **Treating payroll as a fixed cost instead of a percentage of revenue.** Dental and medical practices with hygienists, techs, and front-office staff often don't notice payroll creeping past a healthy range until cash gets tight.
- **Letting accounts receivable age without a clear collections process,** especially common in service businesses billing on invoice terms and in medical practices waiting on insurance reimbursement.
- **Church finance teams relying on a single volunteer bookkeeper** with no second set of eyes on monthly giving trends or restricted fund tracking.

None of these mistakes are about effort. They're about not having the right numbers organized the right way, on a schedule that matches how fast the business actually moves.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Post

**Q: What should a monthly financial dashboard include?**
A: A useful dashboard tracks revenue against prior periods, gross margin by service line, cash on hand, accounts receivable aging, and payroll as a percentage of revenue. The goal is a five-minute read that shows trends, not just a snapshot of raw numbers.

**Q: How often should a small practice review its financials?**
A: Monthly, not quarterly or annually. Waiting until tax season to review performance means several months of decisions get made without accurate information, which is when margin problems and cash shortages tend to go unnoticed.

**Q: Why do optometry and dental practices need industry-specific accounting?**
A: Practices have unique cost structures, like lab fees, frame inventory, and insurance reimbursement timing, that generic bookkeeping doesn't capture well. Industry-specific expertise catches margin erosion and billing issues that a general accountant might miss.

**Q: What does outsourced accounting cost compared to hiring in-house?**
A: Outsourced accounting is typically billed as a fixed monthly fee rather than an hourly rate or a full-time salary, giving practices predictable costs and access to CPA-level insight without the overhead of an in-house finance department.
